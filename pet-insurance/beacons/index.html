Category Landscape — What You're Looking At

The category has a visual problem. Every brand looks like it's selling something you'd rather not think about.

Stock photography as the default
Happy dogs on white backgrounds. Smiling families. None of it feels specific or owned. The photography is interchangeable across brands — you could swap the logos and nothing would break.
Blue is the category color
Every brand here defaults to blue — trust blue, healthcare blue, authority blue. None of them have figured out that the color itself signals "this is a financial product you'd rather not need."
Fear is the acquisition hook
The implicit message across all five sites: something bad is coming. Get ready. That's the default register of the category, and exactly what the strategist brief is asking us to leave behind.
Lemonade is visually distinct
Hot pink, bold type, irreverent copy. It reads differently from everything else here — which is why it earned its place in the beacon section below. But the visual differentiation is doing more work than the product thinking, at least on the marketing site.
Section 02
Insurance Reimagined
We looked at the insurance app landscape broadly. Lemonade was the only brand worth designating as a beacon — because it's the only one that treats the claim flow as a design problem rather than a compliance obligation. The chat interface doesn't always hold up under legal copy, but the underlying decision is right. The question for this project is where to go from there.
Lemonade
Multi-line insurance · 2 flows: Filing a Pet Claim, Plans in Progress
Filing a Claim (Pet)
Plans in Progress
The claim flow runs long — but it's a chat, so each screen is just one question. It feels specific and conversational rather than heavy.
What to steal
  • 1One question at a time, in a chat thread. What would be a 15-field form becomes a conversation. The cognitive load is the same. The felt experience isn't. The key is routing logic written around the pet's situation, not the claim category.
  • 2The pet's name appears constantly. Not "your pet" or "the covered animal." Hiromi. "How is Hiromi doing now?" Every question is about Hiromi specifically. It's a small thing that changes the register of the whole flow.
  • 3"I promise to claim only what I truly deserve." Before any details are entered. An honesty commitment upfront — reduces fraud and also signals that the brand trusts you. Worth the friction.
  • 4No progress bar, no step counter. Progress is the accumulating chat thread. Milestone labels — "DOCS SUBMITTED," "IN REVIEW" — appear inline. Forward momentum without a tracker.
  • 5"$1.8M donated to 59 charities this year" lives on the product dashboard. Not in a marketing email. In the app. That number doing trust work where the policy is already paid for is worth noticing.
Category Synthesis — Insurance Reimagined

The claim flow is where trust is made or broken.

The claim is the product, not the exception
When a pet needs care, the experience of filing a claim is the most important thing the app will ever do. Design it accordingly. Most insurance apps treat it like a legal obligation.
Voice recording is an open lane
Lemonade asks for a voice note describing what happened. Gen Z communicates by voice DM. No other insurance app in the category has picked this up.
The disclosure doesn't have to break the design
The fraud warning in Lemonade's flow is jarring because it's unstyled — it looks like a different app. Legal requirements can still be designed.
Color as a category signal
Lemonade's hot pink says "this isn't your parents' insurance" before a word is read. The new brand needs something that does the same work against the parent brand's clinical palette.
Section 03
Digital Finance for Gen Z
The design logic across these apps is consistent enough to read as a shared philosophy: the number belongs to you, color signals meaning, plain language lives where you need it most. None of it is complicated. None of it has made it into insurance yet — which is the gap this project is working in.
Chime
Neobank · 2 flows: Home, Paying Someone
Home
Paying Someone
Chime reads like it's on your side. That's a small thing that most financial apps don't bother with. Worth paying attention to how they do it.
What to steal
  • 1The balance owns the home screen. Nothing competes with it. Just the number, huge, at the top. The premium amount or coverage limit in an insurance app could take the same position.
  • 2The Next button stays gray until the input is valid, then snaps to green. No error state, no warning copy — just color doing the work. Clean pattern for quote flows and claim forms.
  • 3"Let's review" instead of "Review your payment." One word. The first version sounds like a person. The second sounds like a system. That gap matters in a claims context — "Let's make sure everything looks right" versus "Review and submit."
  • 4The fraud warning gets a full dedicated screen. Plain language, not fine print. A hard stop before the action completes. Insurance has the same opportunity before a large claim submission — design the disclosure instead of dumping it at the bottom of a form.
  • 5The "What's this for?" field has a curated emoji tray — ❤️ 💸 🔥 🎉. It renders inline in the confirmation. The equivalent in a claim flow: "What happened?" with 🐾 🏥 💊 turns a required field into something a bit more human.
Cash App
Peer-to-peer payments · 2 flows: Home, Transferring Money
Home
Transferring Money
Cash App has a specific visual personality and doesn't apologize for it. Color is doing brand work here, not just decoration.
What to steal
  • 1The green is streetwear green, not fintech green. It communicates something about who uses this app before a word is read. Whatever color system this brand chooses, it should do the same — not default to the category's visual language.
  • 2"Turn spending into status." The rewards promo doesn't say "earn points." It's making a statement about identity. The equivalent for insurance isn't hard to find — covering your pet says something about who you are.
  • 3When you send money, the entire screen turns green. Chrome, keypad, buttons — all one color. The action becomes an event. The enrollment confirmation screen in an insurance app could do the same thing. Right now it's usually a gray form summary.
  • 4"Sent! Will be deposited once [name] accepts." It says what's actually happening. Not "Transaction submitted." For claims: "Claim received. We'll review within 2 business days." Don't claim resolution that hasn't happened.
Wealthsimple
Investment platform · 2 flows: Home, Buying Crypto
Home
Buying Crypto
The most thoughtful financial app in this set about how money feels, not just how it works.
What to steal
  • 1There's an eye icon next to the balance. Tap it and the number disappears. Discretion as a first-class feature. Coverage amounts carry the same emotional weight — worth acknowledging that not everyone wants them on screen all the time.
  • 2The Crypto section has a different header color. Lavender, not green. It says "you're somewhere else now" without a full redesign. If this app ever adds wellness or a different coverage tier, that's a pattern worth borrowing.
  • 3The risk disclosure is a full screen with an illustration and plain language. "Our advice? Do your research." Brand voice in a compliance moment. Pre-existing conditions and exclusions in an insurance app don't need to read like a terms of service page.
  • 4The confirmation screen shows "$0.09 CAD — 2% of order amount." Not buried, not in a tooltip. Right there. Gen Z specifically doesn't trust products that hide how they make money. Show the math.
Category Synthesis — Digital Finance

What these apps are doing that insurance isn't.

The number is the hero
Premium amount, deductible, claim payout — these should sit at display scale with nothing competing. Not in a form row. Not in a table. Just the number.
Color changes when something important happens
Enrollment, first coverage activation, claim approved — these screens should look different from everything else. Color as an event signal, not just branding.
Copy that sounds like a person
"Let's review." "Turn spending into status." Writing toward the feeling, not the function. Every anxiety moment in the insurance flow has an equivalent opportunity.
Trust copy at the moment of friction
Not in a privacy policy. Not linked. Right there, next to the field causing the hesitation. When someone pauses at the vet history question, that's where the plain language goes.
The next step is visible after completion
Post-enrollment: "View your policy" and "Add another pet." Post-claim: "Track this" and "File another." The success screen is already the next entry point.
Section 04
Delight-Driven Life Apps
These apps were built for the in-between moments — after the initial decision, before anything goes wrong. That's the design challenge most relevant here: what does the app feel like on a Tuesday when nothing is happening? None of the insurance apps we looked at have a good answer to that. These do.
Calm
Mindfulness · 2 flows: Home, Playing Sleep Content
Home
Playing Sleep Content
Calm's home screen is a mountain lake. Nothing is happening. You haven't done anything yet. It still feels like something worth opening.
What to steal
  • 1The mountain lake photo is the first thing you see. Not a headline. Not a CTA. Just the image. It says something before any copy has to. For an insurance brand, leading with a photo of the pet — just the pet, at home — would do the same work.
  • 2"Still awake?" After a sleep story ends, Calm asks a human question. No form, no system message. Just a check-in. The equivalent after a claim submits: "Still have questions?" beats "Claim received — reference #82847."
  • 3When you're in a session, the chrome disappears. Just the audio and the sky. For the claim filing flow, the same principle applies — the moment is stressful enough. Strip everything that isn't necessary to get through it.
  • 4"Get lazy." A card that meets you where you actually are, not where you're supposed to be. That's the tone. Not aspirational. Not therapeutic. Just honest about the moment.
Duolingo
Language learning · 2 flows: Completing a Lesson, Streak
Completing a Radio Lesson
Streak
Duolingo makes repetition feel like it's working in your favor. Worth studying for how it handles the gap between doing a thing once and making it a habit.
What to steal
  • 1The "+15 XP" is visible before you enter the lesson. The reward is surfaced at the entry point, not held back as a surprise. Any recurring action in this app — logging a vet visit, checking coverage, submitting a wellness receipt — could show the payoff upfront.
  • 2When you get a streak of correct answers, the bar label changes to "COMBO x2" and turns green. One copy and color swap. The bar goes from passive tracker to live feedback. A wellness streak in an insurance app could do the same.
  • 3The streak is a calendar, not just a number. Months of marked days turn the habit into a record you actually own. A monthly view of vet visits and check-ins would work the same way — absence reads as motivation, not failure.
  • 4Friend streaks show a name and a flame count, no ranking. It's ambient, not competitive. "Your friend logged a vet visit this week" is a soft nudge.
Headspace
Mindfulness · 2 flows: Today, Completing a Session
Today
Completing a Session
There's a dotted timeline in the session view. Three dots: one checked, one active, one empty. It shouldn't work as well as it does.
What to steal
  • 1There's a dotted timeline in the session view — completed step gets a green check, active step gets the only orange dot on screen, upcoming step is empty. Three dots, one color. A simple path showing where someone is in coverage setup, or what's coming up for their pet this month. The anxiety it quiets is "am I doing this right?"
  • 2When you finish a session, a checkmark appears on the timeline. That's it. No confetti, no modal, no points. The check is the confirmation. When a claim is approved, a check and a dollar amount. That's the whole screen.
  • 3"457 meditating" — and the number changes mid-session. It goes to 447. That's how you know it's live. "847 claims processed today" would be operationally credible in a way "trusted by millions" never is.
  • 4The feed is organized by time of day, not content category. "Start your day." "Your afternoon lift." "At night." No data required — just an acknowledgment of where you are in the day. Headers like "Right now," "This week," "Coming up" would do the same work in an insurance home screen.
Notion
Productivity · 2 flows: Home, Creating a Page
Home
Creating a Page
Notion solves the blank-canvas problem. A new user lands with something already there — structure, suggestions, a place to start. Worth thinking about for what happens right after enrollment.
What to steal
  • 1The home screen leads with "Jump back in" — the last thing you touched, as a visual bookmark. Not a dashboard. Not a summary. Your context, immediately. The equivalent in an insurance app: last vet visit, last claim status, next renewal.
  • 2The header says "Alex's Notion." Not "Your Dashboard." Not "Welcome back." Just a name. For this app: "Biscuit's Plan." The app belongs to the pet.

Spotify
Music streaming · 2 flows: Home, 2025 Wrapped
Home
2025 Wrapped
Wrapped turns annual billing into something people look forward to. The insurance annual renewal is the same moment — a recurring billing event — and it's currently the most dreaded communication in the category.
What to steal
  • 1Wrapped opens with "You listened. We counted." One screen, no data. Just copy. Then the numbers arrive. The staging makes each one land harder. An annual insurance review could work the same way — start with "You had a big year, Biscuit" before you show anything.
  • 2"Cloud State Society." Spotify takes listening data and gives you a fictional tribe membership. The insurance version isn't hard to imagine: "The Overprotective Optimist — 0 claims filed, coverage checked 14 times." Or applied to the pet: "Biscuit is a Chaos Specialist. She's in good company."

Strava
Fitness tracking · 2 flows: Home, You (Profile)
Home
You (Profile & Progress)
Strava shows you what your progress will look like before you've made any. The structure is there on day one. Worth borrowing for the post-enrollment empty state.
What to steal
  • 1"🥇 Kudos on your first activity!" appears as a card in the feed, not a modal. It stays there. Inline, permanent. First claim filed, first year of continuous coverage, first wellness check — these could all live as cards in the pet's timeline rather than screens that pop up and disappear.

Category Synthesis — Life Apps

What these apps understand that insurance doesn't.

Start with an image, not a headline
Calm's mountain lake does emotional work before any copy appears. For this brand: lead with the pet. A photo of someone's actual dog at home says more than any tagline.
Strip the UI at stressful moments
Calm removes its chrome during a session. Filing a claim is already stressful. Remove the nav, the banners, the extras. Just the flow.
Progress can be quiet
Headspace's three dots and Duolingo's streak calendar show where you are without making it a performance. A simple path — here, next, done — works better than a progress bar.
Copy that meets you where you actually are
"Get lazy." "Still awake?" These are honest about the moment rather than aspirational about it. The insurance equivalent: "Filing a claim is stressful. Here's how it works."
Data can describe identity
Wrapped doesn't report your listening habits — it tells you who you are as a listener. An annual insurance review could do the same: what kind of pet parent are you, based on how you've actually used this.

Onboarding Reference
Ten different first-time experiences, and a clear pattern across the strong ones: they ask for something before they ask for anything. Goal first, information second. The user's commitment builds before their patience runs out. The sequencing matters more than the number of steps.
Chime
Banking that has your back
  • →Every input is a question, not a label. "What's your legal first name?" instead of a field called "First Name." Across 34 screens that accumulates into something that feels less like a form.
  • →Trust copy lives where the hesitation lives. The pattern throughout this flow: reassurance text sits adjacent to sensitive fields, not in a footer or linked policy page. Right there, at the moment someone pauses.
  • →The feature carousels come after you've signed up, not before. SpotMe, Credit Builder — introduced once you're already in. Build some trust before asking someone to care about features.
Cash App
Identity creation, not account creation
  • →Creating your $Cashtag feels like making a social profile, not opening an account. Enrolling a pet in insurance is the same reframe — not "create a policy," but "create a profile for someone you love."
  • →The Cash Card screen lets you customize a physical Visa card mid-onboarding. It makes the product feel real and owned before you've used it. A coverage card or pet profile card could do the same thing for insurance.
Wealthsimple
Intent-first, portfolio reveal as payoff
  • →The flow is structured around your goal, not the compliance checklist. Personal information comes after intent is established. The equivalent for insurance: ask what the user is worried about before asking for vet history or payment details.
  • →The data collection builds toward something visible. Questions accumulate into a personalized output — not a generic summary screen. The quote reveal should feel the same way: "here's what we built for your specific dog," not a premium on a gray form.
Lemonade
Maya, conversation UI, quote as relief not bill
  • →"Hi, I'm Maya." An AI avatar named Maya. It reframes data collection as talking to someone. The question for this brand isn't whether to do this — it's how the character behaves at the moments when someone is stressed.
  • →The quote is designed as a reveal, not a form output. The price arrives after a conversational flow, not at the top of a comparison table. That sequencing changes how the number lands.
Calm
The product before the pitch
  • →The first screen is a breathing animation. No account creation, no pitch. You experience the thing before you're asked to sign up for it. Every other app explains what it does. Calm just does it.
  • →The trial paywall is explicit about the timeline. What you get, when the reminder comes, when it ends — laid out plainly. The insurance equivalent: "Today: you're covered. Day 14: first bill. Cancel any time before that."
Duolingo
Goal-first, commitment language, no account until the end
  • →Account creation comes at the end, not the beginning. By then you've set goals, picked a level, practiced. You have something to protect. Abandonment at the sign-up screen drops because you've already invested.
  • →The daily goal screen says "I'm committed," not "Next." A small declaration. For insurance enrollment: "Protect Biscuit" works better than "Continue."
  • →"10 min/day = 50 words in your first week." An abstract commitment translated into a concrete outcome. For insurance: "Dogs like Biscuit need emergency care about 1.4× in their first year. Your plan covers it."
Headspace
Breathing first, personality reveal as post-paywall payoff
  • →"Thinking carefully about which course is right for you…" Full-screen cobalt. White type. No spinner. A loading state treated as a moment. "Building Biscuit's plan…" with a simple full-bleed treatment is the same move.
  • →The quiz produces "Calm Explorer" — a named identity type with an illustration. Your answers describe who you are, not just what you want. Answering questions about your pet and how you care for them could produce the same thing: a pet parent type that feels real and shareable.
Notion
"Think it. Make it." — short, direct, pre-populated home
  • →"Think it. Make it." Two verbs. No adjectives. It makes a promise and assumes you're capable. The copywriting goal for this brand is somewhere in this register — not "protection for what matters most," something that treats the user as already competent.
  • →New users land with content already there. A Getting Started template, four pre-built pages. The dashboard isn't blank. For insurance: a wellness checklist, a sample claim path, an upcoming reminders placeholder — the shape of the thing before any data exists.
Spotify
Brand restraint, the artist grid reveal
  • →The artist grid going from gray placeholders to real photos. A functional loading moment becomes a small reveal — the grid transforms from abstract to personal as images populate. The equivalent for insurance: the pet profile building from fields to a full card with the pet's photo.
  • →"Millions of songs. Free on Spotify." Three things in one sentence: abundance, price barrier removal, brand name. The tightest value prop sentence structure in this set. The equivalent for this brand needs to be this economical.

  • →Non-binary and "Prefer not to say" sit at the same visual weight as Man/Woman. No call-out, no badge. Just there.
  • →Real athletes, real moments, no sets. The photography is alive. A golden retriever mid-leap does more brand work than a color system or an illustration style.
Cross-Brand Onboarding Synthesis

What the first screen should do.

Start with the pet
Every strong flow leads with the user's goal. First screen: "Who are we protecting?" Then the pet's name on every screen after that. "What's Luna's birthday?" "Has Luna seen a vet this year?" "Luna is covered." The app belongs to the pet.
Make the quote reveal feel like something
After collecting breed, age, health history — show "Dogs like Luna have a 1-in-3 chance of emergency care this year. Your plan covers up to $X of that." The price lands differently when it follows a forecast, not a form.
Trust copy lives next to the thing causing anxiety
Not linked. Not in a footer. Right there, adjacent to the field. At payment: "Your info is never sold." At vet history: "This helps us quote accurately, not deny claims." At paywall: "Cancel any time before [date]."
The collected data should build something visible
After 5 questions, Wealthsimple shows you a real personalized portfolio. The quote reveal should feel the same way: "here's what we built for your specific dog" — not a generic premium on a gray screen.
Show the product before asking for an account
Calm opens with a breathing exercise. Duolingo asks what language you want to learn before you sign up. By the time the user is asked to commit, they're already invested.
No insurance app opens with full-bleed pet photography
Most look like health insurance with a dog illustration. The gap is closer to what Strava does: real animals, real moments, alive. Not stock, not illustrated — someone's actual phone roll. That, combined with a pet-name-first conversational flow, would already be different from everything else in the category.
Section 06
Tone & Aesthetics
Not apps. Brands. Six references for what this product should feel like when nothing is happening and the user has no reason to open it — and opens it anyway.
The Brief
Why Gen Z sincerity, not Millennial sarcasm
The target user has already made the decision emotionally — they're not shopping, they're confirming. That's a different brief than acquisition. The brands in this section don't sell to their users; they reflect them back. Gen Z specifically tends to respond to that register better than to irony or aspiration. The reference point isn't "we'll take care of you" — it's closer to "you already know what you're doing, here's a product that agrees."
Visual feel
Warm, editorial, lived-in
Real photography. Animals in actual homes. Unposed. Not stock, not hero shots — closer to someone's phone roll.
Copy register
Direct, sincere, no winking
Short sentences. Present tense. Names the real thing without euphemism. Assumes the user is already capable and already cares.
Color world
Warm neutrals + one confident accent
Cream, warm white, sage or terracotta as the base. One accent color that marks key moments. Not clinical blue. Not a full system.
The register to avoid
Fear-based, transactional, or ironic. Most insurance brands default to the worst-case scenario as the acquisition hook. This brand shouldn't.
The register we're after
Warm, direct, no performance. The user is already invested. The product's job is to be easy to trust — not to convince them they made the right call.

Tone & Aesthetics Synthesis

The brand that's already there, before anything goes wrong.

Don't perform sincerity — just be direct
These brands don't try to seem warm. They just say the thing plainly. No hedging, no exclamation points, no "we're here for you." The directness is what feels warm.
Terracotta, cream, sage — one accent
The palette of someone who takes good care of things. One specific color that activates at enrollment, claim approval, and annual review. Everything else stays neutral.
Recognition over aspiration
These brands make you feel like the person you already are, not the person you could become. The user is already a good pet parent. The product just makes it visible.
The quiet moments are the brand
Parachute works best when nothing is happening. So does this app. The space between emergencies — a renewal notice, a vet visit summary — needs to feel like a Sunday morning, not a health portal.
Real photography, no sets
A golden retriever asleep on a bed. A cat on a kitchen counter. Unposed, alive, from someone's phone. Every brand in this section uses real photography in actual contexts. This one should too.
Copy that trusts you
Graza, Maude, Brightland — none of them over-explain. They name the thing and move on. "We pay your vet directly. In seconds." That's the register. Not "peace of mind." Not "protection for what matters most." Just the fact.
