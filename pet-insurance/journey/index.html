01 Emily
The audience
Emily.
24. New dog. Phone in hand.
Figuring it out in real time.
CityAustin, TX — renting
Income$52K. Thinks carefully about money.
DogFrench Bulldog mix. 9 weeks old. Unnamed.
ResearchChatGPT, Reddit, TikTok. Not Google.

"I don't even know what I don't know yet. I just want to not mess this up."

She decided to adopt three weeks ago. She's already spent $340 on things the internet told her to buy. Pet insurance hasn't come up yet — but it will. The question is where, and from whom.

Arc 01 · Today

What actually happens.

Before the dog comes home

She opens ChatGPT and asks what she needs.

The question isn't "should I get pet insurance." It's "I'm getting a puppy, what do I need to know." A list comes back — food, crate, vet, vaccines, flea prevention. Somewhere in the follow-up questions, the words "pet insurance" appear. Not as an ad. As advice from a system she already trusts.

This is the first moment. No pet insurance brand is designed to be present for it.
Consideration

She ends up on Lemonade. Reddit told her to.

She's not comparing on price. She's comparing on legibility — does this brand feel like it understands her, or is it waiting for her to mess up? Lemonade feels like the first. She enrolls because it's the brand that showed up in the conversation she was already having.

Reddit recommends Trupanion second — "after you've been through something." The new brand needs to earn the first recommendation, not inherit the second.
Enrollment → the quiet middle

She pays $56 a month. The brand disappears.

A policy PDF. A welcome email. Then nothing. Six months later it's Tuesday afternoon and the dog is acting weird. She goes to Reddit. She goes to TikTok. She texts her friend. She does not open the insurance app, because the insurance app has given her no reason to.

The brand earned $56 a month and vanished. The relationship that should have been built in week one has to be rebuilt — if it can be — at the $1,800 bill.
What if the brand had shown up on day one?
Arc 02 · The opportunity

What could happen instead.

Before the dog comes home

The brand is part of the conversation forming the question.

Not a comparison ad. Not a sponsored result. A brand present in the AI conversation, the breed forum, the adoption partner checklist — where Emily is already deciding what kind of pet parent she wants to be. She finds this brand before she knows she's looking for insurance.

Enrollment

She understands exactly what she just bought.

The quote flow shows her what her French Bulldog mix is likely to need, year by year, and what that costs with and without coverage. She commits not because someone reassured her, but because the math is visible and the tradeoffs are named. No fine print. No surprises deferred.

Go Home Day

The dog comes home. So does the brand.

Not a policy reminder. Something that says: we know the puppy just arrived, here's what the first week looks like for a dog like hers. Breed-specific. Useful. The brand earns a relationship before anything has gone wrong — so it has somewhere to stand when something does.

The only uncontested moment in the entire arc. Every competitor's onboarding is a policy document and a thank-you email. This one isn't.
The quiet middle

Tuesday afternoon. The dog is acting weird.

She opens the app — because the app has been useful before. Breed-specific health alerts. A symptom triage that tells her whether to wait or go. The brand that showed up on Go Home Day is the brand she reaches for now. The $1K–$2K fear has somewhere to go other than Reddit.

406 average views per vet fundraising post vs. 6 for pet products. The anxiety is constant. The brand that becomes the exit from the caring loop wins the category emotionally — long before any claim is filed.
Moment of truth

The $1,800 bill. Direct pay. 38 seconds.

VetDirect Pay processes the claim before she checks out. No form. No 30-day wait. She walks out paying her share. This moment is already Trupanion's strongest. What's different is that it arrives inside a relationship — not as a first impression.

Arc 01 · Today

What actually happens.

The renewal email

The bill goes up. The explanation is thin.

A renewal notice arrives. New rate: $64/month. Brief language about age-based pricing. No context about what the $672 paid for in aggregate. No reminder of the coverage she has. No acknowledgment that she hasn't filed a single claim. It reads like an invoice, not a relationship.

The parent brand's BBB average dropped to 1.18/5 on exactly this dynamic. Not the rate hike — the surprise. Emily isn't surprised yet. But she's close.
The comparison moment

She opens the Lemonade app to see what they'd charge.

She's not angry. She's doing due diligence. The same due diligence she did a year ago. Lemonade is still fast. Pets Best is still cheaper. If the brand she's with can't answer "what did I get for $672" — she might not stay.

Renewal is the moment the brand's promise is tested against memory. If there's no relationship to fall back on, it becomes a pure price comparison — and the new brand can't win that fight.
What if she heard from the brand before the bill arrived?
Arc 02 · The opportunity

What could happen instead.

30 days before renewal

The brand reaches out first. With the math.

Not a rate notice. A year-in-review: here's what happened with your dog's breed this year, here's what the coverage was worth in actual claims paid for French Bulldogs like hers, here's what's changing and exactly why. The rate increase arrives with context that makes it legible — not a surprise, an explanation she already has.

The parent brand's failure wasn't the rate hike. It was the silence before it. The mitigation is a transparency layer that runs proactively, before the invoice, every year.
The renewal conversation

She knows what the $672 actually bought.

Claims paid for dogs like hers in her zip code. The one Tuesday night triage that saved her a $400 emergency visit she didn't need to make. The Go Home Day content she still references. The rate increase is visible, contextualized, and attached to a real reason. She renews — not from inertia, but from understanding.

Resolution

She tells someone. Before they've been through anything.

This is the advocacy the new brand is built for — not the shocked-positive post-claim review that Trupanion earns today, but the pre-crisis recommendation. "I've had it for a year, never had to use it, and I still feel like it's worth it." That's the category-shifting word of mouth. It starts here.

The audience that got recognised pre-crisis becomes the audience that recommends the brand pre-crisis to others. Advocacy moves upstream. That's what makes the renewal moment strategic, not just operational.
The design challenge · Renewal

The product vision is clear. The emotional sequence is the work.

Radical transparency — real math, no fine print, honest coverage — is the right promise. But you have to trust someone before their math means anything to you. That trust has to be built before the first claim, reinforced at renewal, and it starts on Go Home Day. The design question is: how does the brand earn that trust in the long quiet stretch between enrollment and emergency — and prove it every year after?

03 Zach
The audience
Zach.
25. First pet of his own.
Toronto. Didn't expect to feel this way about a cat.
CityToronto, ON — renting with a roommate
Income$48K. Cautious with money. Not averse to spending if the value is clear.
CatYoshi. Domestic shorthair, approx. 10 weeks. Male. Shelter intake: healthy.
SourceToronto Humane Society. Adopted Saturday. First vet appointment: next week.

"I've never done this before. I just don't want to make a decision I'll regret because I didn't know something."

He spent two weeks on the shelter website before committing. He read every "first week with a kitten" Reddit thread he could find. He bought cat litter, learned from a TikTok post it was the wrong kind for a kitten, and returned it to the store. He's thoughtful, a little anxious, and paying attention — exactly the person who will enroll in pet insurance if someone explains it to him at the right moment. Nobody has yet.

Arc 01 · Today

What actually happens.

Before Yoshi comes home

The shelter sends a confirmation email. That's it.

He's been approved. Pickup is Saturday. The email lists what to bring and where to park. There's a link to a PDF of Yoshi's intake notes — weight, approximate DOB, intake vaccinations, a note that he was found in good health. Zach reads it twice. Then he goes looking for what he's supposed to do next, because the email doesn't tell him.

The shelter already has everything the insurance enrollment needs to know about Yoshi. That data exists. It just doesn't go anywhere.
The research spiral

Pet insurance comes up. He doesn't understand it. He keeps scrolling.

It surfaces in a Reddit thread — someone in r/cats mentions enrolling before the first vet visit. He clicks through to two or three sites. The quote flows all ask roughly the same questions: breed, age, deductible, annual limit. None of them explain why any of it matters for a ten-week-old kitten specifically. The prices feel arbitrary. He decides to figure it out after the vet appointment.

He's not resistant. He's uninformed — and the category's answer to uninformed customers is a quote form. It's not enough.
The first vet appointment

The vet finds a heart murmur. Grade 2/6. Probably nothing. Worth watching.

The vet is calm, careful, reassuring. Yoshi is likely fine — many kittens present with a functional murmur that resolves on its own. But she wants to recheck at 16 weeks. If it persists, a cardiology referral. Zach nods. He takes the handout. He gets home and Googles "kitten heart murmur cost." He spends an hour doing that instead of enrolling in the insurance he was going to sort out this week.

Any condition identified at the first vet visit is now pre-existing. The window he didn't know existed has closed. The brand that could have warned him — didn't.
The enrollment attempt

He tries anyway. The exclusion is right there in the fine print.

He enrolls two weeks after the appointment. The confirmation email arrives. Somewhere in the policy document: pre-existing conditions are not covered. Cardiac conditions identified prior to enrollment: not covered. He doesn't call to confirm. He pays his first month and files it under "probably fine." The brand has his money and his low-grade distrust.

He's enrolled, which counts as a win in the funnel. But the relationship starts with an asterisk — and he knows it, even if he can't name what it is.
What if the shelter was the first touchpoint, not the last?
Arc 02 · The opportunity

What could happen instead.

The adoption confirmation

The shelter's approval email has one more thing in it.

Not an ad. A single line: "Before Yoshi's first vet visit, it's worth getting pet insurance sorted — anything identified at that appointment becomes pre-existing." And a link. The link passes what the shelter already knows: Yoshi's name, approximate DOB, sex, intake health status, the adopter's city. Zach doesn't fill out a form. He confirms what's already there.

The shelter is a distribution partner, not a referral source. The business argument is B2B2C — and it's a more defensible moat than any SEO strategy. The shelter has the context. The handoff just needs a pipe.
Enrollment

The app opens knowing Yoshi. Zach just has to say yes.

The greeting: "Sounds like Yoshi just came home." The inference log shows what transferred from the shelter — name, age estimate, sex, intake vaccination status, Toronto location. Two questions remain: what's his monthly budget, and has he had a vet appointment yet? He hasn't. That answer triggers the one piece of pre-education that changes everything: enroll now, while the slate is clean. The urgency is honest, not manufactured.

The first vet visit

The murmur is found. He already has coverage. He already knows what that means.

The vet mentions the recheck. Zach pulls up the app on the way home — not to file a claim, but because the brand has been useful before. There's already a card waiting: what a Grade 2 kitten murmur typically means, what the recheck will involve, what cardiology looks like if it comes to that. The anxiety has somewhere to go. The brand that enrolled him before the appointment is the brand he reaches for after it.

The murmur is covered. Not because the product is generous — because Zach enrolled before the appointment. The brand's single most important act of pre-education is: tell him the window exists before it closes.
The quiet middle

16 weeks. The recheck is tomorrow. The brand already knows.

The enrollment date, Yoshi's approximate DOB, and the clinical note from the app together surface a card the evening before: what to expect at the cardiology recheck, what questions to ask, what the two most likely outcomes are and what each means for coverage. Zach didn't set a reminder. The brand did. This is the Wonder Weeks model made operational — not reactive to what he asks, but anticipatory of what he's about to need.

This is the ordinary made concrete. The brand doesn't wait for a claim or a question. It uses what it knows — enrollment date, kitten age, flagged condition — to show up before he has to go looking.
Month six

The murmur resolved. He tells someone at work who just got a kitten.

Not a review. Not a prompted share. Just a conversation — "actually, get the insurance before the first vet visit, I almost didn't." He doesn't remember the policy terms. He remembers that the brand was useful when it needed to be, and that it showed up before anything went wrong. That's the advocacy the category has never earned. It starts with a shelter email and a single line of pre-education.

The design challenge · Zach

Pre-education isn't a marketing problem. It's a timing and channel problem.

Zach would have enrolled before the first vet visit if someone had told him why it mattered — specifically, clearly, at the moment the adoption was confirmed. The shelter already has the context. The brand just needs to be present in the handoff. The design challenge is building the infrastructure for that pipe, and earning enough trust in the 72 hours between adoption and appointment that the enrollment feels like the obvious next step — not a sales interaction. The shelter isn't a partner because they send referrals. They're a partner because they hold the data that makes the first screen feel like recognition instead of a form.

Across all three journeys

Where the work lives.

Primary opportunity

Onboarding as trust infrastructure

Go Home Day is the brand's only uncontested moment and the hinge on which every other phase turns. It's not a CRM trigger — it's the proof point that makes the claim a confirmation and the renewal a conversation. The new brand has no field channel and no 25-year halo. This is the only phase where it can prove itself before it needs to.

Distribution gap

Present where the question forms

For Emily, the first touchpoint is a ChatGPT conversation. For Emily at renewal, it's a comparison tab. For Zach, it's the shelter adoption confirmation. The new brand needs to exist in those moments before anyone is evaluating a quote.

Whitespace

Identity-affirming, not fear-led

Lemonade owns fast and modern. Pets Best owns sensible default. Nobody owns the brand that recognizes the pet parent — at any stage — as someone already doing the right thing. That's the emotional territory across all three stories.
